Research heritage · microbiology · biotechnology · renewable fuels

From yeast microbiology to independent bioethanol production.

ItisScientific's planned renewable ethanol programme builds on founder Costa Arvanitis's background in microbiology, research with Professor Ian Macreadie at RMIT University, recognised undergraduate performance, honours research and peer-reviewed investigations using yeast models.

Research history and planned manufacturing activity — not a verified ethanol production yield

Microbiology research with Professor Ian Macreadie

Costa studied microbiology at RMIT and worked with Professor Ian Macreadie on honours-level research using the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae as an experimental organism. The work involved biochemical hypotheses, microbial growth responses and laboratory investigations of boron-containing antifungal compounds. Costa developed the proposed mechanism and worked collaboratively with Professor Macreadie, who provided scientific supervision, laboratory access and experimental support.

The correspondence from September 2018 documents their collaborative experimental work, subsequent discussion of the results and encouragement to develop a publication. A publisher acceptance letter dated 5 March 2019 confirms that their subsequent paper was accepted after peer review. The final article appeared in the journal's 2020 volume.

Current project: fermentation of 1,500 kg of purchased sugar

ItisScientific reports that it has purchased approximately 1,500 kilograms of sugar for a first practical phase of fuel-ethanol production using the established microbiology of Saccharomyces cerevisiae. The programme is intended to provide a working ethanol supply and operating experience while separately developing longer-term alternative feedstock and acetylene-related research.

Planning estimate · pure sucrose basis
1,500 kg sugar → ~1,020 L theoretical maximum ethanol

For pure sucrose, a complete stoichiometric conversion corresponds to approximately 680 litres of anhydrous ethanol per metric tonne of sugar. Thus the theoretical upper bound from 1.5 tonnes is about 1,020 litres. Actual recovered ethanol will be lower because of substrate quality, organism metabolism, conversion losses and recovery efficiency. The founder's goal of approximately 1,000 litres is a planning ambition, not a verified production result.

Source for the theoretical yield: USDA, Ethanol from Sugar: What Are the Prospects for US Sugar Crops?, Appendix A. Calculations refer to ethanol equivalent, not total volume of fermentation broth.

Phase 1 · Microbial fermentation

Use purchased sugar as a known carbohydrate feedstock to establish a documented production process, record material balance and verify the chemical quality of resulting ethanol. Operational details depend on the actual licensed facilities and applicable approvals.

Phase 2 · Alternative carbon pathways

Evaluate whether non-sugar routes, including the separately proposed calcium-carbide/acetylene research, could offer commercially viable ethanol production. The alternative route has not yet been established as an operating process.

Microbiological expertise helps with culture assessment, recognising contamination, interpreting experimental data and designing reproducible research. It does not remove the need for engineering validation, product testing and regulatory compliance in fuel manufacturing.
